My Life as a Child

I was born in Bluejacket, Oklahoma, on January 17, 1929.

My dad went to college in McPherson, Kansas, to take courses to become a banker. He was employed in a bank in Bluejacket, Oklahoma, before Mignon and I were born. We stayed in Bluejacket for about 13 years before moving back to Kansas. Dad didn’t like working inside, and he wanted an outside job, so we moved to Bartlett, Kansas, where he became a rural mail carrier.

We moved to the country just outside of Bartlett, and Dad bought some farmland, where we planted a large garden. Dad planted corn, beans, and wheat.

Mom washed clothes on a washboard for five kids. We also had no electricity, just lamps to read by and do homework. Mignon and I were chosen to herd the cows along the road to eat grass, and we would throw rocks at each other, sometimes getting hit in the eye.

We were around seven and eight years old when we babysat for a neighbor down the road. Mignon and I had a little straw doll buggy. We would put the baby, Charlott, in the buggy and push her half a mile down the rock road. The buggy’s wheels were bent, but they still worked.

✦

When Mignon and I were just little girls, we lived across the street, in town, from a school janitor. Everyone but the Smith family called him Bum Davis.

One day Mignon and I were outside making mud pies. We started throwing them at Mr. Davis’s porch while we thought he was at work. He came over to us and said, “Do you like mud pies?” We of course said “no,” which was a mistake. He said, “Take a bite out of the mud pies and I won’t tell your dad what you did.” We were happy to hear “won’t tell your dad,” so we took a small bite.

That was the end of making mud pies.

✦

Later, we had chores to do. My dad would take four of us up to the field behind our house, about half a mile away, to pull weeds. Mignon usually stayed at the house and did housework, and sometimes ironing.

When Dad would go on his mail route, he would take his shotgun and shoot rabbits. At home, Mom would clean them. That’s what we would eat for supper.

I remember there was a railroad that ran about 1 1/4 miles behind our house. Hobos would jump off the train and come knocking on our back door for food or work. We always fed them, sometimes inviting them into our house or to sit in the backyard. My dad only picked a few of them to work for him.

He once put two of them upstairs with us four girls. Quinton was away, as he had been drafted into the Army. Can you imagine that now — two men upstairs with three or four girls? I remember two of their names were Walter Wood and Gene Stafford. Everyone liked Gene quite a bit.

It seemed like the Black men were better workers than the white men. My dad hired four of them to build a pond so the cows would have water to drink. When the ponds and wells went dry, we hooked up large barrels on a trailer and went into town to fill them. We would come home, dip the water out of the barrels, and put it in a big trough for the cows. Several times this was at ten or eleven at night, and then it was off to school the next day.

✦

One of the jobs Mary and I had was milking the goats. I usually held Nanny the goat by the horns while Mary milked it. My dad would have Mary squirt the milk in his mouth. Yuck.

✦

My mom would get chicken feed in cloth sacks, about 50-pound bags. The print on the bags had flowers on it. Mom would cut them and make dresses for us four girls. They were really pretty, I guess. We thought we were being punished, because we never bought dresses from the store.

Winter and School Days

We lived a mile from town. In the winter it was very cold, and we had to walk to school. Mom made us very long cotton socks. Only us four Smith sisters had to wear them, so we wore garters made from elastic strips sewn together. We would roll them down below our knees as soon as we got out of sight of the house.

We never got to stay after school and play with the other kids, because we had work to do at home.

There was a very large pond across the road from us. In the wintertime we would all get together, including the town kids, and slide on it with our shoes on. We didn’t have skates. That was lots of fun.

✦

We went to high school in Chetopa, Kansas. Quinton, Mary, and I went there first. Mary and I lived with Grandpa and Grandma Fritz in their funeral home. When Mignon got into high school, she and I rode the bus to Altamont High.

One cold winter day, we rode the bus home, and one of the boys, Richard Graw, started acting up. The bus driver, Mr. Williams, told Richard that if he did not sit down he would put him off the bus. Richard said, “No you won’t.”

Mr. Williams stopped the bus, opened the door, and said, “Get off the bus.” Richard got off about half a mile down the road, and then Mr. Williams got worried. He turned the bus around but couldn’t find him. Because it was so cold, Mr. Williams started to panic. When Mr. Williams got into town, someone else had already picked Richard up and brought him home.

I never heard if the bus driver got into trouble or not. I guess I really didn’t care.

Grandpa & Grandma Fritz

When I lived with Grandpa and Grandma Fritz in their funeral home, Grandpa would send me downstairs where the dead people were kept. I was so scared, and he knew it. He would wait until I was down the stairs, then slip in behind one of the caskets and make a thumping noise. He thought that was so funny.

✦

Also, when Grandpa and Grandma got in the car, Grandpa would act like he was going to drive away after Grandma was halfway in. She would yell, “Charlie, stop!” He was so full of it.

✦

When Grandpa had a couple of Black men helping him move bodies in the hearse, he would shut the men in the hearse just to scare them. They would laugh and say, “Mister Charlie, you are full of shit.”

A Word I Never Forgot

Mom got real sick one day, so I stayed home from school to help her. We had a stove that used propane or butane gas, and Mom told me to go light the stove and warm up some soup. She didn’t know how terrified I was to strike a match.

I started crying, and she got so mad at me that she said I was acting like a “simpleton.” I did not ask her what the word meant, but it has stayed with me forever. I would have called myself something even worse. I think Quinton told me it meant someone was simple. I thought to myself, that does not sound too bad.

Always Afraid of Getting It Wrong

Something no one knew about me: I had no self-esteem. I always had the fear of being called on to do something, because it was always in my mind that I would do it wrong. At school or church, I never volunteered to do anything, with the exception of being a hostess.

When I got up enough courage, I said I would be a hostess. I took pies for dessert, and one of the women offered to help me carry them. She made me drop one of them. So, see, I even messed up through her.

✦

One time, I think I was about eight years old, I was in Sunday school and we were told to learn a Bible verse to say in class. I did not remember to study it. When the teacher, Miss Bertha, an old maid, called on me, I crawled under the table, which of course she told my dad. I always remembered to study after that.

So if I appear to be dumb or forgetful, I just don’t trust myself for anything.

I Love All of My Children and Grandchildren

One day I will rewrite all this so you can read it better. But I do know for sure that I love my children and my grandchildren with all my heart.

Please stick all together and be there for each other.

We all make mistakes.

With Love
